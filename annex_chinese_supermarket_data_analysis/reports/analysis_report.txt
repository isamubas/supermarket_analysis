==============================================================================
Full July-June years (the data begins and ends exactly on this boundary):

Per trading day, FY20/21 -> FY22/23 (day-adjusted, so the closures do not
distort the comparison):
  Revenue      RMB 3,542 -> RMB 3,224   -9.0%
  Kilos        451 -> 536   +18.9%
  Lines        957 -> 796   -16.8%
  Price/kg     RMB 7.85 -> RMB 6.01   -23.4%
  Kg per line  0.471 -> 0.673   +42.8%

Read those five lines together, because any one of them alone misleads:

  * The shop moved +19% MORE produce in year three than in year one.
  * It did so across 17% FEWER scanned lines.
  * Each line got +43% heavier -- fewer, bigger purchases.
  * Average price per kilo fell 23%.
  * Net effect on takings: -9.0% per day.

This is not a demand problem. Volume grew. Revenue fell because price per
kilo fell faster than volume rose -- and margin percentage actually improved
(28.3% -> 32.5%), so the shop is not being
squeezed on markup either. What changed is the mix and the ticket: there are
fewer scanned lines, and each one is heavier and cheaper per kilo.

Whether that means fewer customers or the same customers consolidating their
shopping cannot be settled from this data -- there is no basket or customer
ID in it. Both readings are consistent with what is here, and they call for
different responses, so it is worth resolving before acting on it.

The middle year is the one to be careful with. FY21/22 is the trough on every
measure -- revenue, kilos and lines all bottom out there before recovering.
The four-day closure sits in FY22/23, so it is not what caused the dip; the
dip precedes it. Comparing year one straight to year three skips over a
disrupted middle rather than describing a smooth trend, and nothing here
should be read as three steady years of decline.

==============================================================================
7. THE MARGIN TRAP -- ITEMS THAT LOOK PROFITABLE AND ARE NOT
==============================================================================
The damage from spoilage in this business is broad, not concentrated in a few
rotten lines. Said plainly so the section is not oversold:

  * Only 2 items actually flip from profit to loss once spoilage is
    paid for, and between them they lose RMB 21 over three years.
    As a headline finding that would be trivial, and it is not the story.

  * The real cost is RMB 239,699, spread across the whole book as a
    7.1-point
    haircut on margin. Markups here average about 1.6x, which is wide enough
    that spoilage rarely pushes a line negative -- it just quietly takes a
    fifth of the profit on every line at once.

So the list worth acting on is not 'which items lose money' but 'which items
destroy the most profit through waste'. Those are the big sellers, because
volume multiplies the loss rate:

==============================================================================
11. WHAT WENT WRONG, AND WHAT TO DO ABOUT IT
==============================================================================

1. Footfall is draining away while volume grows
   ----------------------------------------------------------------------
   WHAT THE DATA SHOWS
     Scanned lines per trading day fell 17% across the three years, yet kilos
     sold ROSE 19%. Fewer, larger purchases. Revenue per day still ended -9%
     because price per kilo fell 23%. A shop can survive losing customers
     while each remaining one buys more -- right up until the point it
     cannot.
   WHAT TO DO
     Put lines per day on the same chart as revenue and watch it weekly.
     Revenue held flat here while the customer base eroded underneath it, and
     no revenue-only report would have shown that. If the cause is a
     competitor opening nearby, this is the only number that will say so
     early.

2. Spoilage is not priced in, anywhere
   ----------------------------------------------------------------------
   WHAT THE DATA SHOWS
     RMB 239,699 of book profit over three years never existed -- it was
     stock bought and binned, 19.3% of everything the till reported as
     profit. The correlation between an item's loss rate and its markup is
     +0.047: pricing ignores spoilage completely. A vegetable that bins a
     quarter of its stock carries the same markup as one that bins none.
   WHAT TO DO
     Set price from loss-adjusted cost -- cost / (1 - loss rate) -- then
     apply the target margin. On a 25%-loss line that is about a third more
     markup than today. It is one formula in the pricing sheet and it is the
     highest-value change available in this dataset.

3. Waste is concentrated in the best sellers, not the worst
   ----------------------------------------------------------------------
   WHAT THE DATA SHOWS
     The top 10 items by waste cost carry 44.4% of all spoilage cost. Only 2
     items actually flip from profit to loss -- the damage is volume
     multiplied by loss rate, so it lands hardest on the lines the shop sells
     most of. The two worst (Xixia Mushroom (1) and Broccoli) waste RMB
     40,750 between them -- 3.0x the true profit earned by the entire bottom
     half of the range.
   WHAT TO DO
     Attack the short list, not the long one. For those ten lines
     specifically: smaller and more frequent deliveries, cold-chain and
     handling checks, and loss-adjusted pricing. A single point of loss rate
     recovered on Broccoli is worth more than delisting fifty dead SKUs.

4. The range has a long dead tail nobody has reviewed
   ----------------------------------------------------------------------
   WHAT THE DATA SHOWS
     60 items sold on ten days or fewer in three years, and 110 have not sold
     at all in the final six months. The bottom half of the range contributes
     about 1% of revenue. These are not failing products so much as decisions
     made once and never revisited.
   WHAT TO DO
     Run a quarterly range review with a hard rule: no sale in 90 days is a
     delist unless someone signs to keep it. The gain is not the revenue --
     there is none -- it is the ordering attention and shelf space returned
     to the top decile, which is volume-constrained rather than
     demand-constrained.

5. Clearance works; the ordering that makes it necessary does not
   ----------------------------------------------------------------------
   WHAT THE DATA SHOWS
     5.4% of lines are marked down, overwhelmingly after 19:00 (27% of lines
     in the 21:00 hour). Those markdowns still return 9.7% true margin, so
     the discount mechanism is doing its job -- recovering cash from stock
     that would otherwise be binned entirely.
   WHAT TO DO
     Leave the markdown alone and fix what feeds it. Order to the hour
     profile rather than the day total: a heavy delivery before 09:00 for the
     morning market and a light top-up before 16:00 for the evening rush. The
     evening clearance pile is the visible end of a morning over-order.

6. The last trading hour does not pay for itself
   ----------------------------------------------------------------------
   WHAT THE DATA SHOWS
     After 21:00 the shop takes 2.1% of revenue at 24.9% margin, against
     about 30% through the rest of the day. It is simultaneously the thinnest
     and the least profitable hour, and it is the hour with the heaviest
     discounting.
   WHAT TO DO
     Close at 21:00 and move that staffed hour to the 09:00-11:00 peak, which
     carries a quarter of all revenue at full margin. This is a roster
     change, not an investment.

7. The single biggest sales event of the year is fully predictable
   ----------------------------------------------------------------------
   WHAT THE DATA SHOWS
     8 of the ten best days in three years fall in the week before Chinese
     New Year, and the best single day took 10x the median day. The peak is a
     calendar event, not a weekend effect.
   WHAT TO DO
     Plan it twelve months ahead -- ordering, staffing, cash handling and
     cold storage. There is no forecasting difficulty here: the date is known
     years in advance and the shape repeats in all three years of this data.

==============================================================================
12. WHERE THIS ANALYSIS STOPS
==============================================================================
Stated plainly, because the limits matter as much as the findings:

  * There is no basket or customer ID. Nothing here counts shoppers, measures
    basket size, or says what sells with what. 'Traffic' means lines scanned.

  * There is no stock-on-hand figure. Spoilage is applied as a per-item rate
    from annex 4, not observed day by day, so this report can say what waste
    costs on average but not which delivery went bad.

  * A stockout is invisible. An item that sold nothing on a Tuesday may have
    had no demand or no stock, and this data cannot tell the two apart. Every
    'weak seller' verdict here carries that caveat.

  * Everything below gross margin is absent -- rent, wages, power, transport.
    Gross margin is not profit. This report cannot say whether the shop makes
    money, only which lines and hours contribute most to covering the costs
    it cannot see.

  * The elasticity fits are observational. They rank items; they do not
    predict the result of a price change.
