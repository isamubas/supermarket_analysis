Three branches — Ntinda, Nakawa and Kabalagala — trading through Uganda's financial year. 1.3 million line items, daily stock positions per SKU per branch, supplier deliveries, shrinkage records and the loyalty file. That is roughly the shape of what sits inside your POS and stock system today.

Findings, ranked by what they are worth

Six things the year's data says out loud

Ranked by annual margin at stake, largest first. Every figure below was computed from the transaction and stock files — none of it is assumed.

01
Money on the table UGX 54.9m/yr

Spoilage is concentrated in one department, and the power cuts make it worse

Waste runs at UGX 219.7m a year — 2.3% of turnover. Butchery and fish alone account for UGX 97.8m of it, nearly half, before dairy, bakery and produce.

The cold chain shows up clearly in the daily numbers. On days with a grid outage, chilled and frozen waste averages UGX 1.08m against UGX 385,000 on clean days — every outage day costs about UGX 690,000 in stock that did not survive. Kabalagala, the smallest branch, wastes the most in absolute terms, which points at the chillers rather than at the ordering.

What we would do: order fresh to a demand forecast rather than to last week's number, introduce a timed markdown rather than a bin, and put a cost against the generator decision — right now the outages are being paid for in meat instead of fuel.
02
Money on the table UGX 53.9m/yr

You run out of stock exactly when your customers have money

UGX 455m of demand walked out of the door unserved — 4.8% of turnover, and 6.0% of the gross margin you actually earned. It is invisible in the till roll, because a sale that never happened leaves no record. It only appears when you join sales to daily stock.

Lines are out of stock 1.6% of SKU-days on average. In the payday window — the 25th to the 3rd — that rises to 2.4%, against 1.3% through the rest of the month. Nearly twice as bad in the eight days that carry 38% of your turnover. The cause is mechanical: reorder points are set off an annual average that knows nothing about the 28th.

What we would do: re-cut reorder points for the top 60 lines with a month-end uplift, and hold a standing pre-payday order with the three suppliers who cause most of the gaps.
03
Control risk UGX 26.5m/yr

One branch, one till, one shift — three signals that all point the same way

Unexplained stock variance — loss that is neither sold nor recorded as waste — runs at 1.99% of sales at Kabalagala against 0.36% at Ntinda and 0.27% at Nakawa. Kabalagala is the smallest branch and loses UGX 49.1m, more than the other two combined and tripled.

The loss is not spread evenly. It sits on small, high-value, easily-pocketed lines: spirits and beer, personal care, baby formula, tea and coffee. Alcohol alone is ten times the level of the other two branches.

Independently of the stock records, the till data flags the same branch. One operator voids 7.4% of their tickets against a chain median of 1.1%, and their voids climb to 10.1% after 18:00 once the supervisor has left. The same till fiscalises only 74% of its tickets on EFRIS against a 97% median.

04
Pricing discipline UGX 11.8m/yr

Sugar was sold below cost for three months and nobody noticed

Mill prices moved in November. The shelf price did not catch up until February. For twelve weeks over the busiest trading period of the year, sugar carried a negative gross margin — the store paid customers to take it away.

Sugar is a known-value item — one of the handful of lines a Kampala shopper prices in their head before deciding where to shop. You may well choose to hold the price and absorb the squeeze to protect footfall. That is a legitimate strategy. But it has to be a decision made in November, not a discovery made in June.

What we would do: a weekly cost-versus-price review on the top 30 lines, with an alert the moment landed cost crosses shelf price. Ten minutes a week.
05
Money on the table UGX 2.7m/yr, and a rule worth more

The promotions that made money were the ones your supplier paid for

Twelve promotions ran during the year. Split them by who funded the discount and the pattern is not subtle: the five you funded yourself lost UGX 2.31m between them. The six with real supplier funding behind them made UGX 0.57m.

06
Capital, not P&L UGX 9.5m released

A quarter of the range earns 5% of the sales

165 SKUs — under half the range — produce 80% of sales. At the other end, 89 SKUs make 5.1% of sales while holding UGX 19.0m of stock at cost.

The cause is case packs, not bad buying. A line selling two units a month still arrives in a case of six and still needs a facing, so the shelf holds months of cover. Non-stick cookware sits on 277 days of stock. A baby car seat, 103 days. Hennessy, 68 days at UGX 816,000 of tied-up cash.

What we would do: a range review that asks of each tail line — does it earn its facing, does it pull a basket that would otherwise not come in, or is it just capital asleep? Some slow lines genuinely earn their place. Most of these do not.
The pattern behind the findings

Revenue is not the same thing as profit, and the gap is where the decisions are

Ranked by share of sales, the categories at the top of the store are not the ones paying for it. Cooking oil is the single biggest line on the sales report and delivers less than half its share of the margin. Fresh produce is less than two-thirds the size and delivers half as much again.

Airtime deserves a separate word. It is 4.3% of what the sales report calls turnover and 0.8% of the margin, because a UGX 10,000 top-up earns roughly UGX 350. Booking it on the same line as groceries drags the blended margin from 19.6% down to 18.9% and quietly distorts every like-for-like comparison you make. It belongs in its own report as commission income.

Operations

The roster is flat and the day is not

Trading peaks hard between 17:00 and 19:00 as Kampala comes home, and again all day Saturday. The cashier roster barely moves. Measured properly — baskets per rostered cashier-hour — the evening peak runs at nearly four times the pressure of the morning.

The same flatness shows up across the month: 5.1 baskets per cashier-hour in the payday window against 4.3 through the rest of the month. Saturday takes UGX 37.8m against Tuesday's UGX 21.1m — nearly double the trade on roughly the same cover. Queues at the peak are not a staffing shortage; they are a scheduling one.

The prize

UGX 149.9m a year, on a 19% margin business

Adding the recoverable portion of each finding — conservatively, not the whole loss — gives UGX 149.9m of annual margin, 8.4% of what the chain currently earns. On top of that sits a one-off working capital release from the range review.

Method

What most analyses of a Ugandan supermarket miss

This is the part we would want to talk through with you, because it is where a generic retail analysis goes wrong. None of these are exotic. All of them change the answer.

Traps inside the data itself

  • Voided tickets stay in the export. In our year, 3,718 voids worth UGX 122.7m. The line-item table carries no void flag at all — sum it raw and you have overstated turnover before you start.
  • Returns are negative lines. Sum quantity without checking the sign and both units and margin are wrong.
  • Airtime and Yaka are not turnover. They are commission at 3.5%. Left in the sales line they distort every margin percentage in the business.
  • Margin must use the cost in force on the day of sale, not today's cost. Otherwise the sugar squeeze is invisible.
  • Stockouts leave no trace in the POS. A sale that did not happen has no row. You have to join to daily stock to see it.
  • Shrinkage is two different problems. Spoilage is an ordering and cold-chain issue; unexplained variance is a control issue. Reported as one number, neither gets fixed.

The Ugandan calendar

  • The month runs on payday, not on the 1st. The 25th to the 3rd is 31% of the days and 38% of the turnover. Any weekly average hides it.
  • Three school terms, not one back-to-school. Half the year's stationery sells in three months. Miss the window and the stock sits until the next term.
  • Ramadan and both Eids shift the basket — staples and soft drinks up, alcohol down — and they move each year.
  • Festive December was 13% of the year's sales in one month, at the year's lowest margin.
  • The January 2026 election. Households stocked up 4% above baseline the week before, then trade fell 57% during the week of the vote.
  • Two rainy seasons. Rainy days trade 12.5% below dry days — a real effect that will otherwise be blamed on staff.
  • Load shedding. Outage days cost UGX 690,000 in cold-chain stock.

Tax, tender and compliance

  • EFRIS fiscalisation rates by till and by shift. A 3.6% gap here is a URA exposure, and it is also one of the sharpest fraud signals you have.
  • VAT-exempt versus standard-rated mix. Unprocessed foods sit outside VAT; packaged goods do not. Category margin is meaningless until that is separated — and the treatment of specific lines is worth confirming with your accountant rather than assuming.
  • Mobile money is 37% of tender and rising — 34.5% in the first half, 40.4% in the second. Whoever absorbs those charges is absorbing a growing cost.
  • Card MDR against basket size. Card baskets average UGX 45,651 against UGX 31,559 on cash, which changes whether the fee is worth paying.
  • Cash handling has a cost too — banking, transit, float, and the risk. It is not free just because there is no invoice.

Trade and supply

  • Supplier fill rate, measured. Our worst supplier delivered 76% of what was ordered. "Your service has been poor" is a weaker conversation than "you delivered 81% and it cost me UGX 82.7m."
  • Case-pack size drives dead stock more than buying decisions do. You cannot order seven of something that comes in twelves.
  • Supplier credit against inventory days. Here, 10.6 days of stock against 16.5 days of credit — the suppliers are financing the working capital, which is a real strength worth protecting.
  • Known-value items. Sugar, oil, bread, soap, posho. Customers price these in their heads and judge the whole store on them. Running dry teaches them to shop elsewhere.
  • Promotion funding. Who paid for the discount is the single field that decides whether the promotion made money, and it is almost never in the POS.
Next step

What we would ask you for, and what we would ask you

The analysis above needs six exports, most of which your system already produces: transaction headers and line items, the product and cost file, daily stock by branch, supplier deliveries, the waste and stock-count log, and the loyalty file. Twelve months minimum, so the seasonality is visible.

  1. Where do you think the money is leaking? Owners are usually right about the direction and wrong about the size. We would rather test your instinct than start from nothing.
  2. Which lines do you refuse to be out of stock on? Every store has a handful that define its reputation. We want your list before we produce ours.
  3. Who sets the shelf price, and how often do they look at cost? The sugar finding is a process question, not a pricing one.
  4. How is waste actually recorded today — is it counted, weighed, or estimated at close? This determines how much of what we called "unexplained" is really just uncounted.
  5. Do you have written promotion funding agreements with your suppliers, and does anyone check the rebate arrived?
  6. What happens on the 28th? Do you change the roster, the order, or the float — or does the store take payday as it comes?
  7. Who can void a transaction, and does anyone review the voids at the end of the week?
  8. What would you do with UGX 150m? The findings are worth nothing until they are attached to a decision you actually want to make.
